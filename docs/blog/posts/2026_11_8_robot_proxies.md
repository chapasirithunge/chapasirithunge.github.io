# Robotics as an Experimental Framework for Investigating Evolutionary Morphologies and Exploring Novel Embodied Functions  

Robotics has traditionally drawn inspiration from nature, using biological structures and mechanisms to develop new engineering solutions. However the relationship between robotics and biology can go much further than simply replicating what already exists in nature. Robotics can also serve as an experimental platform for investigating how biological morphologies evolved, understanding the functional consequences of different physical structures and exploring alternative designs that evolution has not necessarily produced.

This perspective is reflected in two recent studies published in Science Robotics: Ishida et al. (2024), Paleoinspired robotics as an experimental approach to the history of life, and Gilday et al. (2025), Embodied manipulation with past and future morphologies through an open parametric hand design. Although these studies investigate different biological systems, I see a common scientific motivation behind them: using robotics not only to reproduce biological functions, but also to experimentally investigate the relationship between morphology, mechanics, and behaviour.       

In Ishida et al. (2024), the authors introduce the concept of paleoinspired robotics, where robotic systems are used to reconstruct and investigate the locomotion of extinct organisms. Fossils provide valuable information about ancient biological structures, but they cannot directly reveal how these organisms moved or interacted with their environments. By developing physical robotic models based on reconstructed morphologies, we can experimentally examine the mechanical implications of different anatomical configurations and test hypotheses about evolutionary transitions. What I find interesting is that robotics provides a way to investigate biological functions that are no longer directly observable.        

In Gilday et al. (2025), a similar experimental approach is applied to dexterous manipulation. The authors present an open parametric robotic hand design that allows different hand morphologies to be constructed and evaluated. Rather than focusing exclusively on reproducing the human hand, the study explores a broader range of configurations, including morphologies inspired by other species and hypothetical designs. This makes it possible to examine how variations in finger arrangement, proportions, and mechanical structure influence manipulation capabilities. More importantly, it opens the possibility of investigating morphological solutions that do not currently exist in nature.       

These studies suggest that biological morphology should not be treated as a fixed design to imitate but as a space of possibilities that can be systematically explored. Evolution has produced an extraordinary diversity of biological forms, but these forms represent only a fraction of the configurations that may be mechanically feasible. Through robotics, we can reconstruct past morphologies, investigate the functional principles underlying existing biological structures, and explore entirely new configurations.        

This also changes how I think about bioinspired robotics. Instead of viewing biological knowledge as something that flows in only one direction—from biology to engineering—I see an opportunity for a more reciprocal relationship. Biology provides hypotheses and inspiration for robotic design, while robotic experiments provide physical evidence that can inform our understanding of biological systems. In this sense, robotics becomes not only an engineering discipline but also an experimental methodology for studying biological form and function.        

This is a broader role for robotics in biological research. Robotics can become an experimental framework for exploring the space of biological possibilities, extending our investigation beyond existing organisms to include both extinct and hypothetical morphologies. This creates opportunities not only to understand how biological functions may have emerged through evolution, but also to discover new embodied solutions that could inform future robotic systems.
